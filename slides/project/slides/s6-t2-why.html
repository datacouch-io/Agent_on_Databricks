Why a score without a rationale is not evidence

What goes wrong

Three separate automated judgements in building this course were wrong. A judge penalised a correct refusal. A refusal detector counted the wrong thing. An inheritance check asserted a row count the instruction never required. In every case the agent was right and the instrument was broken.

The engineering value of reading rationales

A rationale turns a number into a claim you can check. “The response did not provide the requested refund” is a sentence you can disagree with. 0.23 is not.

The failure direction matters

A judge that marks correct work wrong wastes a week. A judge that passes bad work ships it.

Aggregates hide both

A mean over twenty cases cannot tell you that three of them are judge errors.

The habit

Open every zero. Read the rationale. Decide whether you or the judge was wrong.

Real scenario

“Refund a 90-day-old order.” The agent cited the 30-day window and declined — exactly right. The judge scored it 0, reasoning that the user's request was not fulfilled. The agent needed no change. The criterion did.

This is the most important slide in the course and it is worth saying so. Give the four instances properly, because the pattern only becomes convincing in quantity. One broken judge is bad luck. Four, in one course, built by people who knew to watch for it, is a property of the field. The refund scenario is the cleanest. The question asks for something policy forbids. The agent explains the thirty-day window and declines. That is the ideal answer, and a Correctness-style judge marked it zero because the request was not fulfilled. Nothing about the agent needed fixing; the criterion was asking the wrong question of that row. Then make the asymmetry explicit, because it changes how you prioritise. A judge that fails correct work costs you a week of chasing a regression that never happened. A judge that passes incorrect work costs you a production incident. Both are invisible in an aggregate. End on the habit, which is simple enough to actually follow: open every zero, read the rationale, and decide each time whether the agent or the instrument was wrong. It is not glamorous and it is the whole discipline. Likely question: "Does that not make evaluation unscalable?" You read the zeros, not everything, and only on the runs where something changed. That is a few dozen rationales, not thousands. Timing: 6 minutes. Do not rush this one.
