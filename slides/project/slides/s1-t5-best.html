Context, memory and injection — do and don't

Do

Treat every tool result as untrusted input, however authoritative it reads.

Return decided answers from tools — one row, named columns, no raw result sets.

Evict finished sub-task output from context. It is the cheapest optimisation there is.

Summarise at a threshold you chose, and make the summary visible in the trace.

Give retrieval-facing agents read-only identities and keep actions behind a gate.

Track input tokens per trace, not just latency. It is the early warning.

Don't

Don't defend injection with prompt wording alone. It raises the bar; it sets no floor.

Don't let top-k creep upward to “improve recall” without measuring the answer quality.

Don't keep every turn forever and call it memory. That is an unbounded bill.

Don't put long-term memory in the system prompt. Retrieve the relevant facts; do not carry all of them.

Don't assume a big context window removes the trade-off. It removes the hard limit only.

Don't index user-authored text into a corpus an action-taking agent reads, without thinking about it.

The second “don't” is the one to watch for in your own team. Raising top-k from 3 to 10 looks like a recall improvement, roughly doubles cost, and often makes answers vaguer. It is the cleanest example of a change that must be measured rather than reasoned about.

Close Session 1's extended material here. Three lines deserve saying. The callout, because it is a change someone in the room will make next week with the best intentions. Recall feels like an unambiguous good. It is not: more retrieved passages means more plausible-looking material to reconcile, and the model's answer gets hedgier as a result. Measure it. "Don't put long-term memory in the system prompt" catches teams that discover memory and implement it as a growing preamble. Every fact you ever learned about a customer, re-sent on every turn of every conversation. Long-term memory is retrieval: store it all, retrieve what is relevant now. "Don't index user-authored text into a corpus an action-taking agent reads, without thinking about it" is the architectural version of the injection lesson. Support tickets and CRM notes are written by people outside your organisation. Indexing them is often correct — just decide it, rather than inheriting it from a pipeline. Likely question: "Is there a scanner for injected content?" There are classifiers, and they are a useful layer. Treat them as you would a spam filter: they reduce volume, they do not make the parser safe. Timing: 3 minutes.
