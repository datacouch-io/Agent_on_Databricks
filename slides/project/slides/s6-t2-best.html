Evaluation — do and don't

Do

Open every zero and read the rationale. Decide each time who was wrong.

Segment the dataset — answerable and unanswerable questions need different criteria.

Use RetrievalGroundedness early. It needs no ground truth and catches invention.

Write deterministic scorers for anything checkable in code.

Print the results columns before trusting any script that reads them.

Keep the dataset fixed between the runs you intend to compare.

Don't

Don't ship on an aggregate. A mean cannot tell you that three failures are judge errors.

Don't score a correct refusal with Correctness. It will mark it down, correctly by its own rule.

Don't use a cheap model as judge and then trust the variance it produces.

Don't change the prompt and the dataset together. You will not know which moved the number.

Don't assume a score improved because the agent did — segment before you celebrate.

Don't skip the trace on a failing case. It tells you whether retrieval or selection was at fault.

The fifth “don't” is from our own Lab 6B. The aggregate rose from 0.23 to 0.30 and from 0.71 to 0.86, which read as a clear win. Segmented, the gain sat where the previous version already scored 16 of 16 and 12 of 12 — so the headline movement was largely judge noise, not better behaviour.

The callout is the most honest thing in this deck, and it is about a result we got wrong first. Our Lab 6B narrative originally assumed the aggregate would worsen when we introduced a stricter prompt. It improved — substantially, on two scorers. The comfortable move would have been to report an improvement. Segmenting showed the gain concentrated in cases the previous version already passed perfectly: sixteen of sixteen and twelve of twelve on the positive cases. There was very little room for genuine improvement there, which means most of the movement was variance in how confidently the judge expressed an already-passing verdict. Say what that implies: an aggregate can move for reasons that have nothing to do with your agent. If you only ever look at the mean, you will ship changes that did nothing and attribute improvements to work that did not cause them. The other line worth emphasising is "don't change the prompt and the dataset together". Everyone does it once, because adding a case you just thought of feels harmless. It destroys the comparison. Likely question: "Is there a sensible threshold for shipping?" Not a universal one. Ship on a reviewed set of per-case results and a stated list of known failures, not on a number crossing a line.
