Three things to take away

1 — Where a control lives decides who inherits it

In the prompt, it is a request. In your code, it binds your callers. In the data layer or a GRANT, it binds everyone — including clients you will never see.

Demonstrated four times: the £50 gate, the Genie instruction that travelled, the audience filter that did not, and MCP discovery filtered by identity.

2 — The measuring instrument is often the broken part

Six times in this course an automated judgement was wrong, and every one of them misjudged correct work.

A correctness judge penalising a correct refusal. A rationale extractor reporting no failures. An inheritance check asserting a row count. A canary read without its control.

3 — Default to the smaller thing

A workflow before an agent. One agent before a supervisor and workers. A keyword retriever as your control. The constraint — MAX_STEPS, the gate, the scope of a Genie Agent — is usually the engineering, and the capability is usually the easy part.

175

Close on these three and resist adding a fourth. The first is the architectural idea and it is portable well beyond agents. Three locations, three reaches. Every design review question about a control reduces to: if someone wrote a new client tomorrow, would this still hold? The second is the epistemic idea and it is the one that will save them the most time. Four broken instruments in one course, all failing in the same direction — marking correct work wrong. That direction matters: it means the default experience of a failing metric is a week spent fixing an agent that was already right. The habit is small: open the failure, read the rationale, decide who was wrong. The third is the discipline, and it is the one people resist because the tooling makes the larger thing easy. A workflow before an agent, one agent before a supervisor, a cheap retriever as a control. The constraint is the engineering. If you have time for one question, ask which of the three they expect to be hardest to hold to in their own team. The answer is usually the third, and it is usually for organisational reasons rather than technical ones — which is worth naming, because it is a real problem and not a failure of understanding. Timing: 5 minutes.
