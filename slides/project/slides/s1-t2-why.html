Why the loop needs a stopping condition you own

What goes wrong without one

An agent that cannot find an answer does not give up. It tries a different tool, re-reads the same document, rephrases its own query — forever. The first time you meet this, you meet it on a bill.

The engineering value

A bounded loop turns an open-ended process into one with a worst case. You can price it, set a timeout on it, and alert on agents that routinely hit the ceiling.

Cost is bounded

Worst case = MAX_STEPS × tokens per step. A number you can put in a budget.

Latency is bounded

Your p99 stops depending on how confused the model happened to be.

Failures are legible

Hitting the ceiling is a signal: wrong tools, vague instructions, or an impossible question.

Real scenario

A support agent is asked about an order that does not exist. Without a ceiling it calls get_order_status with six invented ids, each one failing, each failure making the next guess look reasonable. With MAX_STEPS = 6 it stops and says it could not find the order.

The model decides what to do next. You decide how long it may keep deciding. That separation is not a safety afterthought — it is part of the design.

This is the first of several slides in this course arguing that the interesting engineering in agents is in the constraints, not the capabilities. Lead with the failure mode because it is counter-intuitive. People expect a model that cannot answer to say so. What it actually does is keep acting, because at every individual step calling another tool looks like a sensible move. The loop has no sense of having been here before. Make the budget point concrete: worst case is MAX_STEPS times the tokens per step, and that is a number you can take to whoever signs off on spend. Without the ceiling, the honest answer to "what will this cost" is "unbounded", and that answer stops projects. Then the operational point, which teams discover late: the step count is a diagnostic. An agent that usually finishes in two steps and suddenly averages five has changed, or its data has. That is a metric worth a dashboard, and Session 3 shows you where to get it. Analogy: a taxi meter with no maximum fare. The driver is not malicious; they are just still driving. Likely question: "What is a sensible MAX_STEPS?" Start at twice your expected worst case and watch the distribution. In the labs it is six, and the happy paths finish in two. Timing: 3 minutes.
