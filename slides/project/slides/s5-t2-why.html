Why MCP changes who can call your tools

What goes wrong if you assume your client is the only one

Your agent checks permissions before calling a tool. Fine — until the same schema is reachable by an MCP client written by someone who never saw your checks. Your validation was never a control; it was a property of one caller.

The engineering value

One registry, many clients. Tools are written once and consumed by agents, notebooks, desktop assistants and other teams' systems — all under the same grants.

Reuse

A tool written for one agent is available to every agent, with no integration work.

Reach

Including clients you did not write and cannot review.

Consequence

Every control must live at the server, because the client is now a variable.

Real scenario

A team registers issue_refund and guards it in their agent's loop. A month later a different team points an MCP client at the same schema for a prototype. The guard does not travel. The grant would have.

MCP is the reason the GRANT matters rather than being a nicety. Discoverability by unknown clients makes platform enforcement the only enforcement.

This slide is the security argument for Session 5 and it follows directly from the protocol existing. Make the inversion explicit. Before MCP, it was defensible to treat your agent code as the control point, because your agent was the only caller. A standard protocol plus a managed endpoint removes that assumption. The schema is now a service, and services do not get to assume who their clients are. The scenario is realistic and the timing is what makes it bite: the second team is not doing anything wrong. They found tools in the catalog and used them, which is the intended behaviour. The guard in the first team's loop simply was not part of what they found. Say the consequence plainly, because it is actionable: if a check must hold for every caller, it belongs in the grant, in the function body, or in the data layer. Anything in the client is a property of that client. This is also the fourth time in the course that location has determined reach — prompt versus code, caller versus space, caller versus index, client versus server. Name the pattern; it is the thing people should take home. Likely question: "Can I restrict which clients connect?" You control identities and grants, not client software. Design as though any MCP client could appear, because that is the point of a standard. Timing: 4 minutes.
