Why you cannot improve what you have not pinned down

What goes wrong without a dataset

You change the prompt, try three questions, and it feels better. Next week someone changes the model and tries three different questions, and that feels better too. Nobody can say whether the agent is better than it was a month ago, and the first regression you discover will be reported by a user.

The engineering value

A fixed dataset turns a subjective argument into a diff. Same inputs, two versions, a table of differences you can read — and a record you can show someone later.

Compare versions

Champion versus challenger, on identical ground.

Catch regressions

The improvement that fixes two cases and breaks three becomes visible.

Evidence for release

“16 of 20, with these two known failures” is a reviewable statement.

Real scenario

In Lab 6B, a prompt change looked like a clear win on the aggregate score — 0.23 to 0.30 on one scorer, 0.71 to 0.86 on another. Reading the per-case results showed the gain was concentrated where the old version was already scoring 16 of 16. The aggregate moved; the behaviour mostly had not.

A dataset is necessary and not sufficient. It makes comparison possible; it does not make the comparison honest. That takes reading the rows.

The scenario is from our own Lab 6B run and it is the honest version of an evaluation story. The aggregate scores improved substantially, and the improvement was not what it appeared to be: once segmented, most of the movement sat in cases the previous version already passed completely. The real change in behaviour was much smaller than the headline number. That is the pattern to warn them about. An aggregate is a weighted average of things you have not looked at, and a change in it can come from genuine improvement, from judge variance, or from a shift in which cases the scorer was confident about.
