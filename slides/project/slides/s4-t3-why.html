Why benchmarks make curation an engineering practice

What goes wrong without them

Curation becomes folklore. Someone adds an instruction, someone else rewords it for clarity, a third person adds an example that contradicts the first — and nobody can tell whether the space is better or worse than it was in March. Quality is asserted in meetings instead of measured.

The engineering value

A suite that runs on demand turns every curation change into a before-and-after number. That is regression testing for a probabilistic component, and it is what lets more than one person safely maintain a space.

Catch regressions

An instruction that fixes one question and breaks two shows up immediately.

Onboard safely

A new curator can change things, run the suite, and see what they affected.

Evidence for sign-off

“18 of 20 benchmark cases pass” is a sentence a governance board can act on.

Real scenario

Adding an instruction to exclude test accounts fixed the customer-level questions and broke a count-of-all-accounts case that legitimately wanted them. Without a suite, that trade would have been discovered by whoever relied on the second question, weeks later.

Every instruction you add should arrive with a case that proves it. Curation without measurement is just opinion with a console.

The folklore failure in the first panel is worth describing in full because every team recognises it once named. Three people curate a space over six months, each change locally sensible, and the net effect on quality is unknown. There is no artefact that records whether the space got better. The regression example in the scenario is the honest one: a good instruction broke a legitimate question. Excluding test accounts is right for customer-level analysis and wrong for a count of all accounts. Neither instruction is a mistake; the interaction is. That is precisely the class of problem a suite finds and a reviewer does not. The third panel is the one to mention if there are managers in the room. "Eighteen of twenty cases pass, here are the two failures and why" is a sentence that gets a space approved for wider use. "It seems pretty good" is not. The closing line is deliberately blunt and fair: curation without measurement is opinion with a console. Say it, then soften it by noting the suite does not need to be large. Twenty well-chosen cases covering your distinct question shapes is plenty. Likely question: "How often should it run?" On every change to the space, and on a schedule, because the underlying data changes too. A case that passed on last month's data can fail on this month's. Timing: 4 minutes.
