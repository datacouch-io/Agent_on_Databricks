Step 2 is a tool call

Workers are exposed to the supervisor as tools. Routing is therefore the same mechanism as everything else in this course — a schema, a description and a model's choice.

Step 3 is where the risk is

Whatever the supervisor leaves out, the worker will never know it was missing. Trace the handoff payload, not just the final answer.

Step 5 can loop

A supervisor may consult a second worker after reading the first's answer. Bound that loop, as in Session 1.

The reassuring observation is in the first panel: routing is not a new mechanism. Workers are tools from the supervisor's point of view — a name, a description, a schema — so everything you learned in Session 2 about tool descriptions applies directly to routing quality. A badly described worker is routed to badly. Step three is the one to instrument. Trace the handoff payload explicitly, as its own span. If you only trace the final answer you cannot distinguish "the worker was wrong" from "the worker was given half the question", and those have completely different fixes. Step five is worth a caution. Supervisors that can consult workers repeatedly need a step ceiling for exactly the Session 1 reasons, and the ceiling now bounds a tree rather than a line — so the worst case multiplies faster than people expect. On frameworks: say plainly that this is where they earn their keep. LangGraph and the Databricks LangChain integration give you the graph, the state handling and the handoff plumbing. And say why we built the loop by hand first — so that when the graph does something surprising you know which of these five steps you are looking at. Likely question: "Should workers share state?" Prefer explicit handoffs over shared mutable state. Shared state makes the system harder to reason about and reintroduces the coupling you split to avoid. Timing: 4 minutes.
