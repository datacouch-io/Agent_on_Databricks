Five steps, and step one is the one teams skip. Set @previous before you split, not when you need it. Session 6 made the argument; this is where it applies. The right-hand panel is the discipline that makes a canary an experiment rather than a vibe check. Choose the metric and the threshold before you split. Refusal rate, p95 latency, a groundedness score over sampled traces — any of them is defensible, and choosing after you have seen the data means choosing the metric that supports the conclusion you already reached. Write it down before you start. Point out that both halves are traced, which is the advantage over a black-box A/B test. You can compare which tools each version selected and what retrieval returned, so when the challenger is worse you can say why rather than just that. The f-string gotcha is small and real: on serverless, a backslash inside the expression part of an f-string is a SyntaxError, so building a multi-line report inside one fails. The lab precomputes the string with percent-formatting. Thirty seconds, and it saves someone a confusing error. Likely question: "How long should a canary run?" Long enough to cover a full traffic cycle — at minimum a business day, ideally a week if weekday and weekend differ. Shorter windows measure the time of day. Timing: 4 minutes.
