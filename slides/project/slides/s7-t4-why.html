Why freshness is a commitment you should state

What goes wrong

Nobody agrees a freshness target, so there is no definition of stale, so there is no alert — and the first time anyone measures the lag is during the incident review for an agent that quoted a withdrawn policy to a customer.

The engineering value

A stated target turns an invisible property into a monitorable one. “Policy answers reflect changes within four hours” is testable, alertable, and something the business can accept or reject.

State it

In hours, per data domain. Policy and pricing are not the same as a product catalogue.

Monitor it

Write a known-change canary document and assert the agent sees it. Alert when it does not.

Deletions too

Retired documents are the common silent bug. Test removals, not just additions.

Real scenario

A clause is withdrawn. The source table is updated; the index syncs nightly; a response cache holds yesterday's answer. For up to two days the agent cites a policy that no longer exists — accurately quoting a document that is still in the index.

Freshness is part of safe rollout. A deployment you can roll back in seconds is small comfort if the data behind it is two days old.

The practice here is simple and almost nobody does it. State a freshness target, in hours, per data domain. Not one number for everything — a product catalogue can tolerate a day, a pricing or policy corpus often cannot tolerate an hour. Once it is stated, stale has a definition, and anything with a definition can be alerted on. The monitoring technique in the middle panel is the practical trick worth writing down: keep a canary document whose content you control, change it on a schedule, and assert that the agent's answer reflects the change. If it does not, your pipeline is broken somewhere in the chain and you now know before a customer does.
