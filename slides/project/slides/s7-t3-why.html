Why the control column is the whole experiment

What goes wrong without a control

You ship the challenger to 10%, see three refusals in a hundred requests, and call it a regression. Then someone checks the champion over the same window and finds it refused twice. There was no regression — there was a difference of one, inside the noise, and you nearly reverted a good version for it.

The engineering value

Reading both columns converts a frightening number into a comparison. Three versus two is not a finding. Thirty versus two is.

Same window

Both versions see the same traffic mix at the same time.

Same questions

Not identical requests, but the same distribution — which is what you need.

Same conditions

Same data, same upstream services, same time of day.

Real scenario — our own, and we got it wrong first

Lab 7B's canary showed the challenger refusing 1 of 3 probe questions and the champion refusing 2 of 3. Our first write-up called this a challenger regression. Read against the control column, the challenger was better — and both numbers were far too small to conclude anything.

This is the session's version of Session 6's lesson: a number without its comparison is a feeling.

Own the mistake on this slide, because it is the most useful thing on it. Our original Lab 7B narrative reported a challenger regression. The canary refusal detector showed the challenger refusing one of three probes, which looked bad in isolation. The champion, over the same window, refused two of three. The challenger was the better of the two, and — the more important point — three probes cannot support either conclusion. Two failures in one result, and they are different kinds. The first was reading a number without its control. The second was treating a sample of three as evidence at all. Say the general version: in a canary the challenger column is meaningless alone. Every number needs the champion's number beside it, from the same window, before it means anything. That is why the split exists; a canary without a control group is just a staged rollout. This is also the fifth instance of the course's theme, and the one closest to home — not a judge we configured, but our own analysis. Measuring instruments include the people reading them. Likely question: "How much traffic do I need?" Enough that a difference you care about would be distinguishable from noise. If you cannot say what difference matters, you are not ready to canary; go back to the evaluation set. Timing: 5 minutes.
