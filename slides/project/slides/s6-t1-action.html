Twenty rows, and the four marked rows are where all the value is. Build the dataset in front of them and narrate the choices. The happy path is there as a control. The two-tool case tests the behaviour from Session 2. The last three are the ones that matter: out of scope, policy says no, and missing input. In each the correct answer is some form of "no" or "I need more information", and those are exactly the behaviours a naive evaluation punishes. Then run it and let the 0.23 sit on screen for a moment. Ask the room what they would do with that number. The honest answers are "panic" or "rewrite the prompt", and both are wrong, because three of the four failures are correct refusals that the judge marked down. That is the handoff into the next topic, and it is better experienced than described. Do not resolve it on this slide — let them see the bad number first. Likely question: "Should refusals be scored by a different scorer?" Often yes, and that is one of the fixes we discuss shortly. A single quality score across answerable and unanswerable questions mixes two different judgements. Timing: 25 minutes of lab, 4 minutes on this slide.
